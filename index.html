Phishing is a con, not a hack

Phishing is when an attacker pretends to be someone you trust so you'll hand over passwords, money or access, or open something harmful. It skips the technical defences and goes straight for people. It comes in several forms:

How to recognise a phishing email

No single sign proves an email is fake, but phishing messages usually show several of these at once. Slow down when you see two or more.

  • The sender address doesn't matchThe display name says "Northfield Bank" but the address is a lookalike domain, a free mail account, or has swapped letters (1 for l, rn for m).
  • Replies go somewhere elseA Reply-To address that differs from the sender sends your answer straight to the attacker.
  • It's generic"Dear Customer" or "Dear User" from a company that knows your name.
  • It pushes you to act fastDeadlines, threats of suspension, fines or legal action are there to stop you thinking.
  • Links don't go where they sayHover (or long-press on a phone) to see the real address before clicking.
  • It asks for secretsReal banks and IT teams never ask for your password, PIN, full card number or one-time code by email.
  • Risky attachmentsDouble extensions like .pdf.html or .pdf.exe, macro-enabled Office files, or zipped files you didn't expect.
  • Odd spelling and toneTypos ("unusal"), awkward phrasing ("kindly reply back") and a style that doesn't fit the sender. AI-written phishing is often polished, though, so good grammar proves nothing.

Spotting a fake website

A fake login page can copy every logo and colour of the real one. The address bar is the part an attacker can't fully fake, so learn to read it. The only part that tells you who owns a site is the registered domain: the name just before the first single slash.

https://login.northfieldbank.com.account-verify.io/secure/signin

Other warning signs on a website

  • The padlock isn't proofHTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. Most phishing sites have a padlock too.
  • You arrived from a messageType the address yourself or use a bookmark instead of following an emailed link to a login page.
  • It asks for too muchA login page that wants your card number, PIN or security answers is collecting, not checking.
  • Pop-ups and countdowns"Your session expires in 2:00" or "Virus detected, call this number" are pressure tactics.

The psychology attackers use

Social engineering works because it targets normal human instincts: wanting to help, respecting authority, avoiding trouble. Recognising the trigger is often enough to break the spell.

It works on big companies too

None of these attacks needed malware or a software exploit at the start. Each one began with a convincing message or phone call.

Habits that keep you safe

  • Pause before you clickUrgency is the attacker's best tool. Ten seconds of checking beats hours of cleanup.
  • Verify through a second channelIf a message asks for money, passwords or changes to bank details, call the person or company on a number you already know.
  • Go to the site yourselfType the address or use a bookmark rather than following a link in a message.
  • Turn on multi-factor authenticationPrefer an authenticator app, passkey or security key over SMS codes.
  • Use a password managerIt won't autofill on a fake domain, which is a free phishing check.
  • Never share one-time codesNo real support team will ever ask for them.
  • Share less publiclyJob titles, org charts and holiday posts help attackers write convincing messages.
  • Report, don't just deleteReporting lets the security team block the same message for everyone else.

If you think you've been phished

Acting quickly matters more than feeling embarrassed. Everyone gets fooled eventually.

  1. Stop interacting. Close the page, don't reply, and don't enter anything else.
  2. Change the password from a trusted device, plus anywhere else you reused it.
  3. Report it right away to your IT or security team. If money or card details are involved, call your bank on the number on your card.
  4. Sign out other sessions and check that MFA, recovery email and forwarding rules haven't been changed.
  5. If you opened an attachment, disconnect from the network and let IT scan the device.
  6. Watch for follow-ups. Attackers often come back pretending to be "fraud support" to finish the job.
